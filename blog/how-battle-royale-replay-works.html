Inside the Battle Royale Replay: How 100 Players Race in Real Time

Published October 8, 2026 · By the Tiledly Team
A technical deep-dive into the feature that makes solo play feel social

The Problem: Daily Games Are Inherently Lonely

When you finish a daily puzzle game, you typically see a score, maybe a shareable grid of colored squares, and a count of how many days you've kept your streak alive. That's it. The competition exists in the abstract — somewhere, other people are playing the same puzzle today — but you never feel it. You're playing alone against a clock, not alongside anyone.

This is a fundamental tension in the design of daily games. The whole appeal is that you're part of a global community solving the same challenge. But the experience itself is solitary. We wanted to close that gap — to take the data that already exists on our servers (thousands of real runs happening every day) and turn it into something that players could actually see and feel.

The Battle Royale Replay was our answer. And building it required solving a few genuinely interesting problems.

The Technical Foundation: Real Data, Anonymized Ghosts

The core insight behind the replay is simple: we don't need to invent competitors. We already have them. Every day, thousands of players submit their completion times to the global leaderboard. That data forms a rich statistical distribution — a realistic portrait of how the day's player population performed.

When you finish a game and launch the Battle Royale Replay, the system pulls the current day's score distribution from the leaderboard API. It then generates 99 ghost racer profiles, each assigned a completion time sampled from that real distribution. This is not random invention — it's statistical sampling from actual human performance data. The result is that the field of 99 competitors in your race reflects, with genuine fidelity, the spread of times real players achieved on that same puzzle.

Privacy by design: No individual player's data is ever surfaced. Ghost racers are assigned anonymous procedurally generated names and avatars. The score distribution is used as a statistical input only — no personal identifiers are ever attached to ghost racer behavior.

Faster players in the real leaderboard produce faster ghost racers in your replay. If today's puzzle was particularly tough and the global average time was high, your ghost field will reflect that difficulty. Your race is always contextually accurate to the day's actual competition.

How the Canvas Animation Works

The replay renders on an HTML5 Canvas element. Each of the 100 racers — 99 ghosts plus your own avatar — is represented as a horizontal lane in a track visualization. Progress along the track corresponds to percentage completion of the run: 0% at the start, 100% at the finish line.

The animation engine translates each racer's assigned completion time into a real-time-weighted progress curve. A racer assigned a 14-second completion time doesn't suddenly appear at the finish line at T+14s — they advance continuously across the canvas, with their speed calculated to deliver them to 100% at precisely their assigned time. This creates fluid, visually meaningful racing rather than a series of jumps or teleports.

Your own avatar's progress is derived from your actual run data — the same millisecond timestamps recorded during gameplay. If you hesitated on tile seven, your racer slows slightly. If you had a fast opening sequence, your racer surges. The animation is, in the most literal sense, a replay of what your hands actually did.

Elimination Waves: Manufacturing Tension

A 100-player race that simply runs to completion in silence would be watchable but not exciting. We needed dramatic structure. That's where elimination waves come in.

At regular intervals during the replay, the slowest-performing racers are eliminated from the field — their lane collapses, a brief animation fires, and the remaining field tightens. Early eliminations are large batches (cutting the field from 100 to 60, then 60 to 30). Late eliminations are surgical — one racer at a time, as the final survivors jostle for position near the finish line.

The elimination timing is tuned so that the final 10–15 seconds of any replay feel genuinely tense regardless of where you placed overall. If you finished in the 40th percentile, you'll see yourself survive several early waves before being eliminated. If you finished in the top 5%, you'll watch the field thin around you and find yourself racing toward the line alongside a handful of other fast completers. The structure rewards skill but gives every player a dramatic arc.

Your Avatar, Your Real Run

The key to making the Battle Royale Replay feel authentic — rather than just visually interesting — is that your avatar is not a simulation. It is you. The exact sequence of taps or clicks you made during your run, timestamped to the millisecond, drives your racer's movement on the canvas.

This means the replay is genuinely replayable in an interesting way. You can watch it multiple times and notice where you lost ground, where you made up time, and how small hesitations compounded into ranking differences. It's as close to watching a replay of your own gameplay as a tile-based browser game can produce.

PRO members get a distinguishing visual element: a gold crown above their racer avatar. It's a small detail, but it's earned — it signals to anyone watching a shared replay that this player supports the game that makes the whole thing possible.

Why It Works: Social Proof Through Visualization

The Battle Royale Replay solves the loneliness problem of daily games not by adding real-time multiplayer — which would require infrastructure investment that raises costs and introduces latency — but by making the result of real competition visible. You aren't racing live against 99 players. You're racing a statistically accurate reconstruction of what those 99 players actually did today.

The effect on player experience has been striking. Players tell us the replay is the moment the game clicks — when it stops feeling like a personal score-chasing exercise and starts feeling like participation in something communal. That's exactly what we were aiming for, and it's what drives us to keep building.
